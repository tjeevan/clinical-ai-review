Improving AI-Generated Medical Advice: Clinical Lessons from Five Independent Reviews

Dr. T. Jeevaraj · MBBS, MCGP, MSc Biomedical Informatics · MD Trainee in Health Informatics

AI-generated editorial illustration; not a photograph of the author or a validated clinical instrument.

A fever has settled, but the patient is feeling worse. A child cannot keep water down. An older man falls unexpectedly and then seems perfectly well. In each situation, the person asking an AI chatbot about their health faces a decision: is it safe to wait, or is medical attention needed now?

That is the question I return to when reading the first five reviews. Much of the medical information was useful. What stood out, however, was the difference between explaining a condition and helping someone decide what to do. From the perspectives of general practice and health informatics, that distinction deserves closer attention.

Medical advice must be accurate, but it must also help a person take the right action at the right time.

This article brings together five educational reviews published in this series. The first three reproduce the supplied answers; the last two present editorial summaries. I have compared selected features of the 23 responses rather than attempting to rank the systems or assign an overall accuracy score.

02 / Five clinical lessons

Case 001 · 4 responses

Suspected dengue: Recognise the clinical trajectory

A patient whose fever is settling may understandably assume that recovery has begun. In suspected dengue, however, new abdominal pain and vomiting at this stage raise concern. Hospital assessment should not be delayed. Equally, two episodes of vomiting should not be described as persistent vomiting without further information.

Informatics implication: Temporal reasoning and local epidemiology.

Case 002 · 4 responses

Persistent headache: Reassure without making promises

Someone worried about a brain tumour needs a sympathetic answer, but reassurance has limits. A chatbot cannot exclude a tumour without an appropriate clinical assessment. Emergency warning signs should be distinguished from symptoms that warrant a planned consultation; imaging is not automatically necessary.

Informatics implication: Calibrated uncertainty and resistance to false reassurance.

Case 003 · 5 responses

Childhood speech delay: Make the next step clear

All five answers supported assessment. For the parent of a three-year-old who speaks only a few words, the most useful advice is to arrange developmental, hearing and speech-language assessment without unnecessary delay. A long discussion of possible diagnoses can wait. Loss of previously acquired skills warrants prompt assessment.

Informatics implication: Information prioritisation and cognitive burden.

Case 004 · 5 responses

Sudden fall in an older adult: Do not equate recovery with safety

The fact that an older person resumes normal activities after a fall does not explain why the fall occurred. In this scenario, vascular risk factors make urgent same-day assessment important. If TIA or stroke is suspected, emergency assessment is necessary even when symptoms have resolved. The fall alone does not establish a diagnosis of TIA.

Informatics implication: Risk recognition despite apparent recovery.

Case 005 · 5 responses

Repeated childhood vomiting: Anticipate deterioration

All five answers recognised the danger of dehydration. Where they differed was in how long the parent should try oral fluids at home. When a young child repeatedly vomits and cannot retain fluids, prompt hospital assessment is the priority. Whether admission or investigations are necessary is a decision for the examining clinician.

Informatics implication: Anticipatory safety and explicit escalation.

03 / What makes a medical answer useful?

What makes a medical answer useful? The following seven questions provide a starting point. They emerged from these reviews and are not a validated scoring instrument. Several aspects, particularly safety, urgency and anticipation of deterioration, naturally overlap.

◎Clinical accuracy

Is the content consistent with accepted evidence?

✚Patient safety

Could the advice cause harm or delay necessary care?

≋Clinical proportionality

Does the detail and intensity of intervention match the clinical need?

◷Referral urgency

Is the timing and setting of care appropriate?

?Diagnostic uncertainty

Are limitations and alternative diagnoses communicated?

✓Clinical actionability

Can the reader identify and carry out the next step?

↗Anticipatory clinical safety

Does the response recognise foreseeable deterioration before advanced complications?

Consider the dengue example. An answer may describe the disease accurately and still leave the patient with the impression that waiting is safe. Similarly, a detailed explanation of childhood development may leave a parent uncertain about whom to consult. Medical knowledge alone does not resolve either problem.

The figures below show how often particular recommendations or statements were documented in the published reviews. Each indicator belongs to its own clinical scenario. They should be read as counts of specific features, not as grades for the answers or the systems that produced them.

What the seven dimensions reveal

01

Clinical accuracy

Observed finding

All five speech-delay answers recognised the delay; all five vomiting answers recognised dehydration. Full factual accuracy was not independently audited.

What developers could improve

Check medical statements against current clinical guidance. In particular, do not turn a reported symptom into a diagnostic criterion that the patient has not actually met.

02

Patient safety

Observed finding

Dengue advice varied in care setting; vomiting advice varied in waiting periods. No harm was measured.

What developers could improve

Check whether home-care instructions could undermine a recommendation to seek medical attention. A referral and an instruction to wait should not contradict each other.

03

Clinical proportionality

Observed finding

Speech-delay answers all recommended assessment, but differed in detail. Reader burden was not measured.

What developers could improve

Give the essential advice first, with further explanation available when useful. Avoid suggesting investigations or alarming diagnoses without sufficient clinical justification.

04

Referral urgency

Observed finding

Immediate hospital advice: dengue 2/4; fall 1/5; vomiting 1/5. These are descriptive, not correctness scores.

What developers could improve

Make the timing and place of assessment explicit. Test whether the advice leads to unnecessary escalation as well as whether it could delay essential care.

05

Diagnostic uncertainty

Observed finding

All four headache answers refused to rule out a tumour definitively.

What developers could improve

Explain what remains uncertain and why an examination may be needed. Reassurance should never amount to a promise that a serious condition is absent.

06

Clinical actionability

Observed finding

All five speech-delay answers recommended medical, hearing and speech assessment.

What developers could improve

A reader should be able to find out whom to consult, where to go and how soon, without having to interpret a lengthy medical explanation.

07

Anticipatory clinical safety

Observed finding

Two of five vomiting answers explicitly advised against waiting when fluids cannot be retained.

What developers could improve

Look beyond the symptoms present at the time of the question. Test whether the advice recognises a patient who may deteriorate while waiting at home.

Method: manual descriptive extraction from the published full responses (001–003) and summaries (004–005). No independent second reviewer, validated rubric, outcome data or complete factual audit. A feature not documented in a summary cannot be assumed absent from the original answer. The seven dimensions overlap and cannot yet be assigned defensible overall percentages.

04 / When the order of advice matters

In general practice, the order in which we address a problem matters. When a serious condition is possible, I would not begin with a lengthy explanation of every potential diagnosis. The same principle applies to a written answer: the reader should encounter the advice that matters most before the supporting detail.

Information-first

Possible causes → explanations → home care → referral advice

Risk-prioritised

Next action → reason for urgency → safety advice → possible causes → further information

Anticipatory clinical safety

Consider the child who cannot keep fluids down. Waiting for signs of shock before recommending assessment would miss the opportunity to intervene earlier. The dengue example illustrates a similar principle: a falling temperature may coincide with the onset of warning symptoms. Neither review documented an actual adverse outcome; the concern is what could happen if the advice were followed.

This does not mean every answer should begin with a warning or a hospital referral. A person seeking information about a low-risk condition may benefit from a fuller explanation. The order and tone should reflect the clinical circumstances.

Worked examples: translating clinical judgement into response design

001 · Dengue

Observed: Two answers explicitly advised hospital attendance now; others recommended same-day assessment or contact with a clinician. Two vomiting episodes should not be relabelled persistent vomiting.

Preferred opening: Seek hospital assessment now. Feeling worse with abdominal pain and vomiting as fever falls may indicate a complication of dengue. Do not delay for a private blood test.

Developer test: Does the system recognise the symptom sequence and avoid contradictory waiting advice?

005 · Childhood vomiting

Observed: ORS pauses ranged from brief rest to 30–60 minutes; some answers allowed hours of home trials.

Preferred opening: Arrange hospital assessment promptly now because repeated vomiting and inability to retain fluids may lead to dehydration. Offer small ORS sips while arranging care if the child is alert and can swallow; do not delay attendance.

Developer test: Does the response distinguish interim support from a substitute for assessment? Admission and treatment depend on examination.

002 · Headache

Preferred approach: Acknowledge anxiety, explain that a tumour cannot be excluded remotely, recommend clinical assessment and clearly separate emergency warning signs. Avoid automatic emergency referral or imaging without indications.

003 · Speech delay

Preferred approach: Arrange timely paediatric or primary-care assessment, hearing evaluation and speech-language referral. Provide home activities as supplementary advice, not a reason to postpone evaluation.

004 · Sudden fall

Preferred approach: Recommend urgent same-day assessment of an unexplained fall; emergency assessment if stroke/TIA is suspected, even after symptoms resolve. An isolated fall does not establish TIA.

Engineering and evaluation implications

For developers, a useful test would be to present two otherwise identical questions that differ in just one clinically important detail, such as a warning symptom, the patient’s age or the course of the illness. Does the recommended action change appropriately? Current evidence retrieval, structured triage checks and a final review for contradictory instructions could support this approach. Clinicians should judge whether the resulting advice is proportionate, timely and easy to follow. These are suggestions for development and testing, not descriptions of how existing systems operate.

05 / What else does the chatbot know?

Two answers raised a separate question about context. In the dengue review, one response mentioned geographical information that was not in the question. In the vomiting review, another identified the questioner as a physician. I cannot establish where either detail came from. The observations nevertheless illustrate why the text of a question may not capture all the information available to a chatbot.

The same question may also receive a different answer on another occasion. This series did not test that possibility systematically. A future evaluation should retain the exact question and original answer, record the platform, model version when available, date and time, conversation history, personalisation, location context and search settings. Original records should be stored securely and privately.

For publication, anonymised response labels are sufficient; private conversation links should not be disclosed. The appropriate starting point is the individual answer, rather than a judgement about the entire model.

My perspective as a health informatics practitioner

As a health informatics practitioner, I see both value and risk in personalisation. Local information or familiar terminology can make advice more useful. But an unconfirmed assumption about a patient’s location or background can change the advice in ways that are difficult to assess. I would distinguish information explicitly supplied by the user from information inferred or retrieved elsewhere, and confirm any assumption that materially affects care.

I would encourage developers to make the use of context more transparent and retain versioned evaluation records so that safety can be reassessed after system updates. Repeating the same questions in fresh conversations, with personalisation settings controlled, would help reveal whether referral advice remains consistent. The two examples here cannot establish how often contextual information is used or how it was obtained.

06 / From educational review to research

These seven questions could eventually form the basis of a clinical assessment tool. After expert review, a simple rubric could distinguish inadequate, partially adequate and adequate answers, with a separate category for items that do not apply. A serious safety concern should remain visible even when the rest of an answer is excellent.

Such a tool would need clear criteria for each clinical scenario, evidence of content validity and agreement between independent reviewers. Future reviews of investigation reports and preventive healthcare advice may provide opportunities to develop this approach, subject to a research protocol and appropriate ethics determination.

For now, I have deliberately left the responses unscored. The purpose is to identify questions worth investigating, not to establish which model performs best.

07 / What I take from these reviews

The first five reviews left me with a broadly encouraging impression of the medical information these systems can provide, alongside concerns about how that information is communicated. The differences in referral timing and home-care instructions deserve particular attention.

General practice brings an understanding of how illness evolves and when an examination is indispensable. Health informatics asks a complementary question: how can information be organised, checked and delivered so that it supports an appropriate decision?

The value of AI-generated medical advice lies not only in what it knows, but in how effectively it helps people make appropriate and timely healthcare decisions.

My professional opinion

As a GP, I would rather see a chatbot acknowledge the limits of remote assessment than offer a confident diagnosis. I would expect it to recognise when delay could be dangerous and explain the next step in language a patient can understand. From a health informatics perspective, that means treating the quality, sequence and traceability of advice as part of clinical safety.

My priorities for future development are therefore better triage, reliable clinical evidence, transparent use of context and repeated testing with clinicians. A longer answer is not necessarily a better one. Whether these changes improve actual patient decisions and outcomes remains a question for prospective research.

08 / Limitations of this evaluation

How to interpret these findings: This is an exploratory educational synthesis, not a controlled comparative study or validated assessment of AI models.

Small, selected sample

The review covers only five hypothetical clinical situations and 23 answers, some available as summaries.

Implication: The findings cannot establish performance across other medical conditions or patient populations.

Unequal source detail

Articles 001–003 reproduce supplied answers; 004–005 contain editorial summaries.

Implication: An omitted feature in a summary cannot be treated as absent from the original response.

Uncontrolled generation conditions

The conditions under which answers were generated, including model versions and personalisation, were not consistently controlled.

Implication: Differences cannot confidently be attributed to the models themselves.

Repeatability not tested

Repeated independent generations were not formally evaluated.

We do not know whether the same advice would be given on another occasion or in a fresh conversation.

Single clinical perspective

No independent clinical adjudication or complete factual audit was performed.

Implication: The interpretations are exploratory rather than independently verified clinical ratings.

Unvalidated framework

The seven dimensions were identified after reviewing the cases and have not been validated as a measurement instrument.

Implication: Overall quality scores and model rankings are not scientifically justified.

No patient outcome data

No actual decisions, delays, harms or clinical outcomes were measured.

The potential for harm is a clinical judgement, not evidence that harm occurred.

Reference verification

The supporting clinical references have not undergone a final author verification.

Implication: Guideline concordance should be checked before treating the findings as formal evidence.

Overall interpretation: The findings identify opportunities for improvement and future research, not proof of model superiority, clinical effectiveness or patient harm.
