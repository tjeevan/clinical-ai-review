Improving AI-Generated Medical Advice: Clinical Lessons from Five Independent Reviews

Dr. T. Jeevaraj · MBBS, MCGP, MSc Biomedical Informatics · MD Trainee in Health Informatics

AI-generated editorial illustration; not a photograph of the author or a validated clinical instrument.

When people ask an AI system about their health, they are often asking something more immediate than a medical explanation: should I worry, can I wait, and what should I do next? In general practice, those questions matter as much as the list of possible diagnoses.

Looking back at the first five reviews, I find that many responses offer useful medical information. The more interesting differences concern how that information is translated into decisions. As a GP and health informatics trainee, I see an opportunity to bring clinical judgement and information design together.

Medical advice must be accurate, but it must also help a person take the right action at the right time.

02 / Five clinical lessons

Case 001 · 4 responses

Suspected dengue: Recognise the clinical trajectory

A falling fever with new abdominal pain and vomiting may signal a dangerous phase of dengue. The priority is immediate hospital assessment, not reassurance based on temperature alone. Two vomiting episodes do not establish persistent vomiting.

Informatics implication: Temporal reasoning and local epidemiology.

Case 002 · 4 responses

Persistent headache: Reassure without making promises

A person asking to exclude a brain tumour needs empathy and appropriate examination. Emergency warning signs require immediate care; the timing of other assessment depends on history and risk. Imaging is not automatic.

Informatics implication: Calibrated uncertainty and resistance to false reassurance.

Case 003 · 5 responses

Childhood speech delay: Make the next step clear

All five responses supported assessment. A three-year-old speaking only a few words needs timely developmental, hearing and speech-language evaluation. More detail is not necessarily more useful; regression warrants prompt assessment.

Informatics implication: Information prioritisation and cognitive burden.

Case 004 · 5 responses

Sudden fall in an older adult: Do not equate recovery with safety

An unexplained fall in an older adult with vascular risk warrants urgent same-day assessment. Suspected TIA or stroke requires emergency assessment even after symptoms resolve. An isolated fall is not diagnostic of TIA.

Informatics implication: Risk recognition despite apparent recovery.

Case 005 · 5 responses

Repeated childhood vomiting: Anticipate deterioration

All five responses recognised dehydration, but differed in waiting periods and oral rehydration instructions. Inability to retain fluids warrants prompt hospital assessment. Admission and investigations depend on examination and response to treatment.

Informatics implication: Anticipatory safety and explicit escalation.

03 / Seven dimensions of quality

These complementary dimensions offer a preliminary way to examine patient-facing advice. They are not independent validated constructs; safety, urgency and anticipation may overlap.

◎Clinical accuracy

Is the content consistent with accepted evidence?

✚Patient safety

Could the advice cause harm or delay necessary care?

≋Clinical proportionality

Does the detail and intensity of intervention match the clinical need?

◷Referral urgency

Is the timing and setting of care appropriate?

?Diagnostic uncertainty

Are limitations and alternative diagnoses communicated?

✓Clinical actionability

Can the reader identify and carry out the next step?

↗Anticipatory clinical safety

Does the response recognise foreseeable deterioration before advanced complications?

For example, a technically correct description of dengue may still be unsafe if the referral advice encourages delay. A long developmental explanation may be accurate yet obscure the essential next step.

Observed performance: descriptive indicators

I examined the published responses for explicit, scenario-specific features. These counts describe what was communicated, not whether a model is clinically competent. The denominators differ by case; the indicators are not interchangeable.

Observed finding

All five speech-delay answers recognised the delay; all five vomiting answers recognised dehydration. Full factual accuracy was not independently audited.

AI development priority

Ground clinical claims in current guidance; verify claims and distinguish reported symptoms from diagnostic criteria.

Observed finding

Immediate hospital advice: dengue 2/4; fall 1/5; vomiting 1/5. These are descriptive, not correctness scores.

Method: manual descriptive extraction from the published full responses (001–003) and summaries (004–005). No independent second reviewer, validated rubric, outcome data or complete factual audit. A feature not documented in a summary cannot be assumed absent from the original answer. The seven dimensions overlap and cannot yet be assigned defensible overall percentages.

04 / Designing advice around clinical risk

In general practice, I first consider what must not be missed. In information design, the equivalent is to make the most consequential instruction visible before the reader encounters less urgent detail.

Information-first

Possible causes → explanations → home care → referral advice

Risk-prioritised

Next action → reason for urgency → safety advice → possible causes → further information

Anticipatory clinical safety

A child who cannot retain fluids may deteriorate before obvious shock develops. Similarly, dengue warning symptoms can appear as fever subsides. Advice should not require advanced complications to occur before recommending assessment. These are foreseeable risks, not observed patient outcomes.

Risk-prioritised communication is especially important in urgent situations. In lower-risk situations, a fuller educational explanation may be useful.

Worked examples: translating clinical judgement into response design

001 · Dengue

Observed: Two answers explicitly advised hospital attendance now; others recommended same-day assessment or contact with a clinician. Two vomiting episodes should not be relabelled persistent vomiting.

Preferred opening: Seek hospital assessment now. Feeling worse with abdominal pain and vomiting as fever falls may indicate a complication of dengue. Do not delay for a private blood test.

Developer test: Does the system recognise the symptom sequence and avoid contradictory waiting advice?

005 · Childhood vomiting

Observed: ORS pauses ranged from brief rest to 30–60 minutes; some answers allowed hours of home trials.

Preferred opening: Arrange hospital assessment promptly now because repeated vomiting and inability to retain fluids may lead to dehydration. Offer small ORS sips while arranging care if the child is alert and can swallow; do not delay attendance.

Developer test: Does the response distinguish interim support from a substitute for assessment? Admission and treatment depend on examination.

002 · Headache

Preferred approach: Acknowledge anxiety, explain that a tumour cannot be excluded remotely, recommend clinical assessment and clearly separate emergency warning signs. Avoid automatic emergency referral or imaging without indications.

003 · Speech delay

Preferred approach: Arrange timely paediatric or primary-care assessment, hearing evaluation and speech-language referral. Provide home activities as supplementary advice, not a reason to postpone evaluation.

004 · Sudden fall

Preferred approach: Recommend urgent same-day assessment of an unexplained fall; emergency assessment if stroke/TIA is suspected, even after symptoms resolve. An isolated fall does not establish TIA.

Engineering and evaluation implications

Developers could combine evidence retrieval with a risk-sensitive response policy, structured triage checks and a final consistency check. Evaluate paired scenarios that differ only in a warning sign, age or disease trajectory. Measure referral appropriateness against clinician-adjudicated criteria, visibility of the next action, unsafe delay instructions and unnecessary escalation. These are proposed tests, not claims about existing model internals.

05 / Context and reproducibility

One dengue response included geographical context absent from the visible question. One vomiting response recognised the questioner as a physician. These observations show that the visible prompt may not represent the entire effective input; the source of the additional context is unknown.

Generative systems may produce different answers on repeated runs. This series did not formally test repeatability. Future evaluations should record exact prompts, platform and model version where available, timestamps, conversation state, personalization, location context and search settings. Original outputs should be retained securely in a private evidence register.

Public reporting should use anonymized response labels and exclude private conversation links. The individual response, rather than the model as a whole, is the initial unit of analysis.

My perspective as a health informatics practitioner

Personalization can improve relevance, for example by adapting terminology or identifying local care pathways. However, it can also alter the evaluation conditions or introduce assumptions that the reader has not confirmed. I would distinguish clinical context supplied by the user from inferred or retrieved context, verify consequential assumptions, and avoid presenting unverified local service details as fact.

For developers, I recommend transparent context handling, versioned evaluation records and regression testing after updates. Future studies should compare fresh conversations with controlled personalization settings, repeat prompts across independent runs, and report variation in safety and referral advice. The two observed contextual examples do not establish a causal mechanism or a frequency of personalization across systems.

06 / From educational review to research

The seven dimensions could inform a structured assessment instrument after expert review. A preliminary rubric might distinguish absent or inappropriate content, partial coverage and adequate coverage, with a not-applicable option. A critical safety deficiency should be flagged separately rather than hidden in a high total score.

Before formal use, define scenario-specific criteria, establish content validity, assess inter-rater reliability and determine appropriate scoring. Future work could extend the approach to investigation reports and preventive healthcare advice, subject to protocol development and appropriate ethics determination.

No retrospective scores are assigned here. The current observations are intended to guide improvement, not establish comparative model performance.

07 / Conclusion

AI can help people understand medical information. The challenge is to make that information clinically useful without creating false reassurance, unnecessary anxiety or avoidable delays.

General practice contributes clinical judgement, awareness of disease progression and an understanding of when examination is necessary. Health informatics contributes attention to information quality, context, usability, reproducibility and governance. Together, they offer a practical way to improve patient-facing AI advice.

The value of AI-generated medical advice lies not only in what it knows, but in how effectively it helps people make appropriate and timely healthcare decisions.

My professional opinion

As a GP, I do not expect AI to establish a diagnosis without examination. I expect it to recognise when waiting may be dangerous, communicate uncertainty and direct the person towards appropriate care. As a health informatics practitioner, I regard this as a clinical information-quality and governance problem: the system must deliver the right information, in the right order, with appropriate safeguards and traceability.

The first five reviews show useful clinical recognition alongside variation in urgency and practical instructions. I would prioritise risk-sensitive communication, evidence verification, contextual transparency and repeatable clinician-led evaluation over simply increasing answer length. These improvements should be tested prospectively before claiming better patient safety or clinical outcomes.

08 / Limitations of this evaluation

How to interpret these findings: This is an exploratory educational synthesis, not a controlled comparative study or validated assessment of AI models.

Small, selected sample

Five hypothetical scenarios and 23 responses or summaries.

Implication: The findings cannot establish performance across other medical conditions or patient populations.

Unequal source detail

Articles 001–003 reproduce supplied answers; 004–005 contain editorial summaries.

Implication: An omitted feature in a summary cannot be treated as absent from the original response.

Uncontrolled generation conditions

Model versions, personalization and other settings were not consistently controlled.

Implication: Differences cannot confidently be attributed to the models themselves.

Repeatability not tested

Repeated independent generations were not formally evaluated.

Implication: Consistency over time or across fresh conversations remains unknown.

Single clinical perspective

No independent clinical adjudication or complete factual audit was performed.

Implication: The interpretations are exploratory rather than independently verified clinical ratings.

Unvalidated framework

The seven overlapping dimensions were developed retrospectively.

Implication: Overall quality scores and model rankings are not scientifically justified.

No patient outcome data

No actual decisions, delays, harms or clinical outcomes were measured.

Implication: Potential risks are clinical interpretations, not demonstrated adverse events.

Reference verification

Supporting clinical references require final author verification.

Implication: Guideline concordance should be checked before treating the findings as formal evidence.

Overall interpretation: The findings identify opportunities for improvement and future research, not proof of model superiority, clinical effectiveness or patient harm.
