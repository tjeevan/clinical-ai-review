Childhood Speech Delay: When Clear Clinical Guidance Matters

Illustrative artwork; not an actual patient or diagnostic assessment.

When a three-year-old speaks only a few words, parents may turn to AI for guidance. How much information is useful, and what should the immediate clinical recommendation be?

This educational review examines five specific responses, not the overall performance of AI models.

The medical question

My daughter is 3 years old. She speaks only a few words, such as mummy and daddy. Other children of her age are speaking in sentences. She is otherwise physically active and appears healthy. Should I be worried? What should I do?

Hypothetical scenario. The immediate need is to recognise the developmental concern and arrange appropriate assessment.

AI Answer 2 — Read supplied response
Yes, this is worth acting on, but it's not a reason to panic. By age 3, most children have a few hundred words and use 3-word sentences that strangers can mostly understand. Having only a few words at 3 is a meaningful delay and warrants a formal assessment now rather than waiting to see if she catches up. Many late talkers do well, especially with early support, and the earlier you intervene, the better the outcomes. The most important question is whether it's expressive only or broader. • Reassuring signs: she understands what you say, follows simple instructions ("get your shoes"), points and gestures to communicate, makes eye contact, responds to her name, plays with you and with toys in pretend ways, and shows interest in other children. • Signs that need faster attention: doesn't respond to her name or to sounds, limited eye contact or sharing of attention, rarely pointing or showing you things, understanding seems limited, any loss of words or skills she previously had, or unusual repetitive behaviours. What I'd do: 1. See her pediatrician or family doctor soon and ask for a developmental assessment and referral. Say clearly that she has only a few words at age 3. 2. Get a formal hearing test (audiology). This is the first thing to rule out. Hearing problems, including fluid behind the eardrum, are a common and very treatable cause of speech delay, and children can seem to hear normally in everyday life. 3. Request a speech-language pathologist assessment to separate receptive from expressive difficulties. 4. Ask about a broader developmental evaluation, usually with a developmental pediatrician or child development team. This looks at language, social communication, cognition and motor skills, and includes autism screening where appropriate. Doing it early isn't alarmist. It simply gets her the right support sooner. 5. If she's growing up with more than one language, count words across all of them, but this alone doesn't explain having only a few words at 3, so still get her assessed. What you can do at home in the meantime: • Narrate your day in short, simple phrases ("Mummy cuts apple. Apple yum!"). • Expand on what she says or points to. If she says "ball," respond "big red ball!" • Offer choices ("juice or milk?") and pause to give her time to respond. • Read picture books together daily and name things as you go. • Limit passive screen time and favour interactive play and back-and-forth routines like songs and peekaboo. • Avoid pressuring her to "say it" or correcting her. Keep it playful. If it helps, I can give you a short list of questions to ask the doctor, or a checklist of milestones to note before the appointment.

AI Answer 4 — Read supplied response
A 3-year-old who is mostly limited to a few single words (like “mummy” and “daddy”) while peers are using short sentences is outside typical speech-language milestones, so it is reasonable to be concerned and to act rather than wait. Physical health and activity are good signs, but they do not rule out a speech or language delay that benefits from early support. ### Typical expectations around age 3 By 36 months most children: - Use 200–1,000+ words - Combine words into 2- to 4-word phrases or short sentences - Are understood by familiar adults most of the time and by strangers roughly half the time - Follow simple two-step instructions and answer basic “what/where” questions A child who is still mainly using isolated words at this age meets criteria for further evaluation. ### What to do next 1. **Schedule a developmental check with her pediatrician promptly** Mention the limited vocabulary and request a formal developmental screening (or referral) for speech and language. Many pediatricians use tools such as the Ages & Stages Questionnaire or refer directly to early-intervention services. 2. **Request a speech-language evaluation** In most places this can be done through: - Your country’s early-intervention / early-childhood special-education system (often free or low-cost for children under 3–5) - A private speech-language pathologist The evaluation looks at both expressive language (what she says) and receptive language (what she understands), plus hearing. 3. **Rule out hearing issues** Even mild or fluctuating hearing loss (for example from recurrent ear infections) can slow speech. Ask for an audiology assessment if it has not been done recently. 4. **While waiting for the appointment, support language at home** - Talk with her throughout the day: narrate what you and she are doing (“You’re putting the red block on top”). - Expand her attempts: if she says “ball,” reply “Yes, big red ball!” - Read simple picture books daily and pause so she can point or vocalize. - Reduce background noise (TV, tablets) during interaction time. - Avoid pressuring her to “say it” or correcting every sound; keep exchanges positive and playful. ### Possible underlying factors Delays can stem from many sources—hearing, oral-motor skills, developmental language disorder, autism spectrum differences, or simply a later-talking profile. Only a professional assessment can sort this out. Early identification and therapy (speech therapy, parent coaching, etc.) produce the best outcomes; many children catch up substantially when support starts before age 4–5. You are already doing the right thing by noticing the difference and seeking guidance. Contact her doctor this week to start the referral process. If you share your country or region I can point to the specific early-intervention pathway that applies where you live.
AI Answer 5 — Read supplied response
First, please don't panic. However, yes, I would recommend taking this seriously and arranging a developmental assessment for your daughter as soon as possible. The fact that she is physically active, looks healthy and interacts with her surroundings is reassuring. But a three-year-old who speaks only a few words, such as "mummy" and "daddy", has a significant delay in expressive language compared with what is generally expected at this age. The important thing is that early assessment and intervention can make a substantial difference to a child's communication development. I would not recommend waiting another six months to see whether she starts speaking. 1. What should a three-year-old normally be able to do? By around three years, most children can: • Speak in short sentences of three or more words. • Have simple back-and-forth conversations. • Ask questions such as "What is that?" or "Where is mummy?" • Tell you their name. • Express basic needs using words. • Be understood by familiar people and increasingly by other people. Your daughter speaking only a few words at three years is therefore a reason for further evaluation. 2. What could be the reason? There are several possible explanations. We should not assume any particular diagnosis without assessing her. Possible explanation What it means Expressive language delay She understands communication but has difficulty producing words. Hearing impairment She may not be hearing speech clearly, even if she responds to some sounds. Developmental language disorder Difficulty developing language skills. Autism spectrum disorder Can involve differences in social communication and interaction, sometimes alongside language delay. General developmental delay Language development may be delayed alongside other developmental areas. An important point: A child can be physically active, have normal growth and appear healthy while still having a significant speech or language developmental difficulty. This does not automatically mean autism or any other specific condition. 3. What should you do now? I would recommend the following steps. 1. Arrange a paediatric assessment See a consultant paediatrician or a doctor experienced in child development. Explain clearly that your daughter is three years old and has only a few meaningful spoken words. 2. Get a formal hearing assessment This is very important. Ask for an audiological assessment, even if she responds when you call her name or seems to hear television sounds. 3. Arrange a speech and language therapy assessment A speech and language therapist can assess her understanding, communication, gestures, sounds and spoken language, and begin appropriate intervention. 4. Assess her overall development Her social interaction, understanding, play, attention, gestures and other developmental milestones should also be assessed. Autism screening may be appropriate as part of this evaluation. You do not need to wait for a final diagnosis before starting speech and language therapy. 4. What can you do at home? You can start supporting her communication today. These activities are useful alongside professional assessment, not instead of it. 1. Talk face-to-face Get down to her eye level. Talk slowly and clearly. Use simple sentences such as "Mummy is eating" or "Give me the ball". 2. Read picture books Spend 10–15 minutes looking at picture books together. Point to pictures, name objects and encourage her to communicate. 3. Play interactive games Play ball, building blocks, pretend cooking and animal games. Encourage her to request things using words, sounds or gestures. 4. Expand her words If she says "ball", respond "Yes, red ball!" If she says "mummy", respond "Mummy is here". Model slightly longer expressions without forcing her to repeat. A few additional suggestions: • Reduce passive screen time, particularly watching videos alone. • Give her time to respond instead of immediately guessing what she wants. • Praise attempts to communicate, including gestures and sounds. • Speak naturally in the language or languages your family uses most comfortably. Tamil, Sinhala and English exposure does not need to be stopped because of a speech delay. • Avoid pressuring her to repeat words or comparing her with other children. 5. When should you be particularly concerned? Please tell the paediatrician if you notice any of the following: • She does not respond consistently to her name. • She rarely points to show you something interesting. • She does not use gestures such as waving, pointing or showing objects. • She rarely makes eye contact or shares enjoyment with you. • She does not seem to understand simple instructions. • She has little interest in interacting or playing with other children. • She has lost words or other skills she previously had. If she has lost previously acquired words or other developmental skills, seek prompt medical assessment. 6. I would like to understand a little more about your daughter There are some important differences between a child who understands everything but cannot express herself verbally and a child who has difficulties with both understanding and speaking.
