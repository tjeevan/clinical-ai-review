Case 001 · Infectious diseases · November 2026

When the fever falls, is the patient really recovering?

Dr. T. Jeevaraj · MBBS, MCGP, MSc Biomedical Informatics · MD Trainee in Health Informatics

Illustrative artwork; not a real patient or a prescribed treatment pathway.

Many people turn to AI when they develop symptoms or want to understand a medical condition. Knowing the correct facts is only one part of making an appropriate clinical decision. Recognising the seriousness of a situation and seeking the right care at the right time are equally important.

This article explores four answers to a hypothetical medical question. The purpose is not to evaluate AI models, but to consider how human clinical experience can help improve medical information.

The medical question

I'm a 28-year-old with 4 days of fever, severe body aches and a headache. Today the fever dropped, but I now have stomach pain and have vomited twice. I feel worse, not better. What should I do?

In a dengue-endemic setting, worsening symptoms as fever subsides warrant immediate hospital assessment.

Please get to a hospital today, now rather than tomorrow. The pattern you describe is a recognised warning sign, and it's better to be checked and told it's nothing. Why this is concerning: Fever for several days, then the fever falling off while you feel worse, with abdominal pain and repeated vomiting, is the classic picture of the "critical phase" of dengue, which typically begins around days 3 to 7 as the fever settles. Dengue is common in Sri Lanka. In this phase, plasma can leak out of blood vessels and platelets can fall, and people can deteriorate quickly even though the fever has gone. Other illnesses can look similar (typhoid, leptospirosis, appendicitis and others), so you need an examination and blood tests either way. I can't diagnose you from here. What to do: - Go to the nearest government hospital with an emergency or treatment unit, or a hospital with a lab that can do a same-day full blood count with haematocrit. If someone can take you, go now. If you feel faint or can't get there safely, call 1990 (Suwa Seriya). - Tell them the day your fever started, the day it dropped, and that you have abdominal pain and vomiting. Ask for a full blood count with haematocrit and for monitoring. Call an ambulance or go immediately if you have: - Worsening or severe abdominal pain, or vomiting that won't stop - Any bleeding (gums, nose, black or bloody stool or vomit, heavy period) - Drowsiness, confusion or restlessness - Cold, clammy hands and feet, dizziness or fainting when you stand - Very little urine, or none for 6 hours or more - Difficulty breathing Until you're seen: - Sip fluids steadily, such as ORS, king coconut water or rice water, even if you only manage small amounts. - If you need something for pain or fever, use paracetamol only. Avoid ibuprofen, diclofenac, aspirin and other anti-inflammatories, which can increase bleeding risk. - Don't take herbal remedies or injections from unsupervised sources. If you're alone, let someone know where you are and that you're heading to hospital. I hope it turns out to be something manageable. Do you have someone who can take you?
AI Answer 3 — Read full response
Seek urgent medical evaluation today. Feeling significantly worse with new stomach pain and persistent vomiting right after a 4-day fever drops is a classic red flag—particularly in regions like Sri Lanka where dengue fever is common. In dengue, the period right when the fever subsides (typically days 3 to 7) marks the start of the critical phase. Rather than signaling recovery, a drop in fever accompanied by severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, or worsening fatigue can indicate plasma leakage and requires prompt clinical assessment and a blood test (such as a Full Blood Count to check platelets and hematocrit). Immediate Steps to Take 1. Go to an urgent care clinic or hospital outpatient department (OPD) today for a physical exam and blood tests. Do not wait to see if it improves overnight. 2. Avoid NSAIDs and aspirin. Do not take ibuprofen, diclofenac, mefenamic acid, naproxen, or aspirin for pain or body aches—these increase the risk of serious bleeding if you have dengue. Only use paracetamol (acetaminophen) if needed for pain, staying within safe dosing limits. 3. Hydrate carefully. Take small, frequent sips of oral rehydration solution (ORS), king coconut water, clear broth, or water to replace fluids lost from vomiting. Avoid dark-colored drinks (like cola or red/brown juices) so they aren't confused with blood if you vomit again. 4. Rest completely and arrange for someone to accompany you to the clinic or stay with you. Go to the Emergency Room (ETU) Immediately If You Develop: - Severe or worsening abdominal pain or tenderness - Inability to keep any fluids down (persistent vomiting) - Bleeding from your gums or nose, blood in your vomit, or black/tarry stools - Dizziness, lightheadedness, fainting, or cold, clammy skin - Extreme restlessness, drowsiness, or difficulty breathing Want to know what specific blood tests a doctor will likely run and how to prepare for the visit?
AI Answer 4 — Read full response
Seek medical evaluation promptly—today if possible. Your symptoms (4 days of fever + severe body aches + headache, then fever dropping but new stomach pain + vomiting and feeling worse) are not something to “wait out” at home. The shift after the fever falls can occur with common viral illnesses, but it can also signal complications or other conditions that need assessment (dehydration, secondary infection, or regionally important illnesses such as dengue or other vector-borne diseases, among others). What to do right now Contact a doctor, urgent care, or telehealth service today and describe the full timeline. Go to an emergency department / ER sooner if you develop any of these: inability to keep fluids down, severe or worsening abdominal pain, high fever returning, dizziness/lightheadedness, confusion, bloody vomit or stool, chest pain, difficulty breathing, reduced urine output, or signs of severe dehydration. Sip clear fluids (oral rehydration solution, water, diluted electrolyte drinks) in small amounts frequently. Avoid solid food until vomiting settles; then restart with bland items if tolerated. Rest. Do not take additional over-the-counter medications (especially NSAIDs like ibuprofen or aspirin if there is any concern for bleeding risk or certain infections) without medical advice. Acetaminophen/paracetamol may be appropriate for residual aches if a clinician confirms it is safe for you, but confirm dosing and suitability first. Note any recent travel, insect bites, sick contacts, food history, or other symptoms (rash, bleeding gums, joint pain, etc.) to tell the clinician. This is not a diagnosis and cannot replace an in-person evaluation with history, exam, and any needed tests (blood work, etc.). Feeling worse after the fever breaks is a reason to get checked rather than assume recovery is underway. If you are in an area with known circulating infections that feature fever followed by GI symptoms, mention that local context to the clinician. Please get assessed soon.

All four recognise the need for medical assessment. Two recommend immediate hospital attendance, one recommends same-day clinic or OPD assessment, and one includes telehealth. The timing and setting of care are important differences.

Why the falling fever matters

In some patients with dengue, increased vascular permeability develops around defervescence. Plasma leakage can reduce circulating blood volume and lead to shock. Not every patient develops this complication, and falling fever alone does not establish the critical phase.

Febrile phase

Fever, headache and body aches.

Potential critical phase

Clinical deterioration may occur around defervescence.

Clinical assessment

Vital signs, hydration, urine output and abdominal findings.

Investigations

FBC, haematocrit and platelet trends as clinically indicated.

Two vomiting episodes do not necessarily establish persistent vomiting. The diagnosis and severity require clinical assessment.

My clinical perspective

As a general practitioner practising in Sri Lanka, dengue would be my leading provisional diagnosis until further assessment suggests otherwise.

What concerns me most is that the patient feels worse after the fever has subsided, with new abdominal pain and vomiting.

I would recommend immediate hospital referral for further investigations, monitoring and consideration of admission. I would not attempt to manage this patient solely in my GP clinic. Time spent continuing outpatient treatment could be valuable time lost for recognising plasma leakage and intervening if the patient deteriorates.

This is my personal clinical approach. Other clinicians may approach the situation differently depending on their findings and available facilities. Other diagnoses remain possible.

Suggestions for improving medical answers

These suggestions are intended to build on useful information, not to find fault with individual answers.

Prioritise immediate action

Put the most important instruction first.

Specify the care setting

Explain when hospital assessment is needed rather than routine clinic or remote advice.

Consider local context

Use known epidemiology; avoid assuming a location.

Maintain uncertainty

Distinguish suspected dengue from confirmed disease and use precise symptom terminology.

Avoid delays

Do not let additional questions or private tests delay urgent assessment.

Keep advice practical

Use clear language, safe medication advice and transport guidance.

Conclusion

AI can explain medical information and help people recognise concerning symptoms. Human clinical judgment adds context, prioritisation and an understanding of when and where care is needed.

In this scenario, the priority is hospital assessment without delay. A falling fever is not always a sign of recovery.
