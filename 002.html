A Persistent Headache: When Reassurance Is Not Enough

Dr. T. Jeevaraj · MBBS, MCGP, MSc Biomedical Informatics · MD Trainee in Health Informatics

Illustrative artwork, not a diagnostic pathway. Brain imaging is only indicated in selected patients.

Many people seek reassurance from AI when symptoms persist. A headache lasting two weeks may cause fear of a brain tumour. How can medical information address that fear without offering false reassurance?

This educational article considers four answers to one hypothetical question, not the performance of AI models.

◉ The medical question

I've had a headache for 2 weeks and I read online it could be a brain tumour. Please tell me I don't have one.

The symptom and the fear both deserve attention.

AI Answer 2 — Read full response
I can't tell you that, because I can't examine you or run any tests. But I can tell you what's true: brain tumours are a rare cause of headache. The overwhelming majority of headaches lasting a couple of weeks come from far more common things, such as tension-type headache, migraine, poor sleep, stress, eye strain, dehydration, caffeine changes, sinus problems, or medication-overuse headache. Reading online about the worst possibility is a very common trap, and it tends to make the worry (and sometimes the headache itself) worse. That said, two weeks of persistent headache is worth getting properly assessed rather than waiting out, mainly so you get a real answer and treatment. You should be seen urgently, the same day, if any of these apply: - A sudden, severe "worst headache of my life" - Headache with fever, stiff neck, or a rash - New weakness, numbness, trouble speaking, confusion, or vision changes (double vision, loss of vision) - A seizure or fainting - Headache that wakes you from sleep or is clearly worse lying down or on waking, with vomiting - Headache after a head injury - Progressive worsening over days, or a new pattern of headache if you're over 50 If none of those fit, it's still reasonable to book a doctor's visit in the next few days. They'll ask about the pattern, check your blood pressure, do a neurological exam and eye check, and decide whether any imaging is actually needed. In most cases it isn't. In the meantime, it can help to note when the headache happens, how long it lasts, what it feels like, sleep, screen time, caffeine, and any painkillers you're taking (frequent use of painkillers can itself keep headaches going). If you'd like, tell me what the headache is like (location, type of pain, timing, anything that makes it better or worse), and I can help you think through what's most likely and what to mention to your doctor.
AI Answer 3 — Read full response
It is completely understandable to feel anxious when a headache lasts two weeks and search results point straight to worst-case scenarios. Health searches tend to surface dramatic possibilities early on, which can quickly turn a lingering symptom into a source of real fear. I can't give you a definitive "no" over a screen—only an in-person exam and, if needed, imaging can do that—but a brain tumour is statistically uncommon as the cause of a headache, even one that has lingered for two weeks. Most headaches lasting around this long come from much more everyday culprits. More Common Reasons a Headache Lingers for Two Weeks - Tension headaches: Muscle tightness in the neck, shoulders, or scalp, often fueled by stress, poor posture, eye strain, or prolonged screen time. They commonly feel like a dull band or steady pressure around the head. - Migraines: Not always a brief, one-day event; migraine patterns can stretch out or cycle over days or weeks, sometimes with throbbing pain, light/sound sensitivity, or nausea. - Medication-overuse (rebound) headaches: Taking pain relievers frequently (often several days a week) can paradoxically trigger recurring or persistent headaches as medication levels wear off. - Sinus issues, dehydration, sleep disruption, or caffeine shifts: Changes in sleep routines, skipping fluids or meals, sinus congestion, or sudden shifts in caffeine intake can keep a headache going longer than expected. Warning Signs That Call for Prompt Medical Attention Because a headache lasting two weeks is worth checking with a doctor anyway—both for peace of mind and to find relief—it is especially important to get evaluated promptly if your headache is accompanied by any of these red-flag signs: - A sudden, severe "thunderclap" headache that peaks rapidly - Fever, stiff neck, confusion, or rash - New neurological changes such as vision changes or double vision, weakness or numbness on one side, trouble speaking, or balance issues - A headache that is steadily worsening, waking you from sleep, or changing noticeably when you cough, sneeze, bend forward, or strain - A new headache after a head injury, or in the context of cancer or a weakened immune system Practical Steps You Can Take Right Now 1. Keep a brief headache log for a few days: Note when it happens, what it feels like, possible triggers (sleep, stress, screens, caffeine), and any pain medication you take. That gives a doctor concrete detail right away. 2. Schedule a routine doctor's visit: Getting a straightforward evaluation is the most reliable way to rule out concerning causes and get a plan that actually stops the pain. ◉ Understanding headache

All four answers avoid absolute reassurance and recognise the need for medical assessment. Their recommended timeframes differ: today or within 24 hours, within a few days if warning signs are absent, a routine visit, or an unspecified timeframe.

Primary headache

Migraine and tension-type headache are common examples.

Secondary headache

May result from infection, vascular disease, medication or intracranial pathology.

Clinical examination

History, blood pressure, neurological examination and optic disc assessment when appropriate.

Investigations

Imaging is guided by clinical findings, not anxiety alone.

Brain tumours are an uncommon cause of headache, but duration alone cannot exclude a serious condition. Anxiety should not be assumed to be the cause.

My clinical perspective

As a general practitioner, I would not assume that a patient with a two-week headache has a brain tumour. Nor would I provide definitive reassurance without a proper history and examination.

I would recommend prompt in-person medical assessment, preferably today. A clinician can assess neurological function and decide whether investigations or brain imaging are necessary.

⚠ Emergency warning signs require immediate assessment

Sudden thunderclap headache, new weakness or speech difficulty, seizures, altered consciousness, or headache with fever and neck stiffness require immediate emergency assessment. Do not wait for a routine appointment or continue an online discussion.

My preference for assessment today reflects the limited information in this scenario, not a universal rule for every two-week headache. Other clinicians may reasonably choose a different timeframe after obtaining a fuller history.

Constructive suggestions

Reassurance without promises

Explain that serious causes are uncommon without claiming to exclude them.

Clear timing

Specify when assessment is appropriate and distinguish emergencies from other warning signs.

Explain examination

Describe why history and neurological findings guide imaging decisions.

Empathy and practical action

Acknowledge fear without dismissing symptoms. Additional questions and diaries should not delay necessary care.

Conclusion

AI can explain common causes and help people prepare for a consultation. Human clinical judgment adds individual assessment and appropriate decisions about investigations. Good medical communication reduces unnecessary fear without creating false reassurance.
